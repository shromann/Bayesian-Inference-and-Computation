Question 1 — Beta-Binomial fundamentals 15 marks

This question tests the most fundamental Bayesian workflow: write a prior predictive integral, update a conjugate prior, and interpret the posterior mean/variance.

Question statement

Let \(y\) be the number of heads observed in \(n\) independent spins of a coin, where the probability of obtaining a head is \(0 \le \theta \le 1\).

a) [5 marks] If your prior distribution for \(\theta\) is uniform on \([0,1]\), derive your prior predictive distribution for \(y\), defined as \(\P(y=k)=\int_\Theta \P(y=k\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,\d\theta\) for \(k=0,1,\ldots,n\). Express \(\P(y=k)\) in its simplest terms.

b) [5 marks] Suppose you assign a \(\Be(\alpha,\beta)\) prior for \(\theta\), and observe \(y\) heads out of \(n\). Derive the posterior distribution for \(\theta\), and show that the posterior mean of \(\theta\) always lies between the prior mean \(\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)\) and \(y/n\). Hint: write \(m=\lambda\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}+(1-\lambda)\frac{y}{n}\).

c) [5 marks] Show that if the prior distribution on \(\theta\) is \(\Unif(0,1)\), the posterior variance of \(\theta\) is always less than the prior variance. Derive the posterior distribution for \(\theta\) in your answer.

Q1(a) — Prior predictive distribution core

What this is testing
Can you average the data model over the prior? This is the "kernel integrates to 1" trick in its simplest form.
Teaching solution
  1. Write the data model. Given \(\theta\), \(y\mid\theta \sim \Bin(n,\theta)\), so \(\P(y=k\mid\theta)=\binom{n}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\), \(k=0,\ldots,n\).
  2. Write the prior. \(\theta\sim\Unif(0,1)\), so \(p(\theta)=1\) for \(\theta\in[0,1]\).
  3. Plug into the prior predictive formula. \[ \P(y=k)=\int_0^1 \binom{n}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\cdot 1\,\d\theta = \binom{n}{k}\int_0^1 \theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\,\d\theta. \]
  4. Recognize a Beta function/kernel. Recall \(B(a,b)=\int_0^1 t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\d t=\frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}\). Here \(a-1=k\Rightarrow a=k+1\), \(b-1=n-k\Rightarrow b=n-k+1\). Thus \(\int_0^1 \theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\d\theta=B(k+1,n-k+1)\).
  5. Simplify using factorial/Gamma identities. For integers, \(\Gamma(m)=(m-1)!\). Also \(\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\). Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} \P(y=k)&=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(k+1)\Gamma(n-k+1)}{\Gamma(n+2)} \\ &=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\cdot\frac{k!(n-k)!}{(n+1)!} = \frac{1}{n+1}. \end{aligned} \]
Final answer: \(\displaystyle \P(y=k)=\frac{1}{n+1},\quad k=0,1,\ldots,n\).

Interpretation: Before seeing data, under a Uniform prior on the coin probability, every possible count of heads is equally likely. This is a nice sanity check: uniform uncertainty over \(\theta\) induces a discrete uniform prior predictive over counts.

Common mistakes
Forgetting the \(\binom{n}{k}\); using wrong Beta parameters (\(k,n-k\) instead of \(k+1,n-k+1\)); or leaving the answer unsimplified when the question asks for simplest terms.

Q1(b) — Beta posterior and shrinkage mean core

What this is testing
Conjugate updating and the shrinkage/convex-combination interpretation of the posterior mean.
Teaching solution

Part 1: Derive the posterior.

Prior: \(\pi(\theta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}\), \(0<\theta<1\). Likelihood: \(L(y\mid\theta)=\binom{n}{y}\theta^y(1-\theta)^{n-y}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi(\theta\mid y) &\propto L(y\mid\theta)\pi(\theta) \\ &\propto \theta^y(1-\theta)^{n-y}\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1} \\ &= \theta^{(\alpha+y)-1}(1-\theta)^{(\beta+n-y)-1}. \end{aligned} \]

This is the kernel of a \(\Be(\alpha+y,\;\beta+n-y)\) distribution. Therefore \(\theta\mid y \sim \Be(\alpha+y,\beta+n-y)\).

Part 2: Show the posterior mean lies between prior mean and \(y/n\).

The prior mean is \(\E[\theta]=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\). The sample proportion is \(y/n\). The posterior mean is

\[ m=\E[\theta\mid y]=\frac{\alpha+y}{\alpha+\beta+n}. \]

Use the hint: write \(m=\lambda\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}+(1-\lambda)\frac{y}{n}\). Solve for \(\lambda\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\alpha+y}{\alpha+\beta+n} &= \lambda\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta} + (1-\lambda)\frac{y}{n} \\ &= \frac{y}{n} + \lambda\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}-\frac{y}{n}\right). \end{aligned} \]

Rearranging gives

\[ \lambda = \frac{\frac{\alpha+y}{\alpha+\beta+n}-\frac{y}{n}}{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}-\frac{y}{n}} = \frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha+\beta+n}, \]

provided the denominator is not zero. Since \(\alpha>0,\beta>0,n\ge1\), we have \(0<\lambda<1\). If the denominator is zero (prior mean equals \(y/n\)), then the posterior mean is also equal to that same value, so it still lies between them.

Conclusion: The posterior mean is a convex combination of prior mean and sample proportion, so it always lies between them.

Intuition
\(\lambda=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha+\beta+n}\) is the prior weight. Larger \(\alpha+\beta\) (stronger prior) pulls the posterior mean closer to the prior mean; larger \(n\) pulls it closer to the data proportion \(y/n\).

Q1(c) — Posterior variance under Uniform prior algebra

What this is testing
Can you compute a posterior variance and compare it to the prior variance using a clean algebraic bound?
Teaching solution

Step 1: Prior and posterior. \(\Unif(0,1)=\Be(1,1)\). Prior variance is \(\Var(\theta)=\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)}=\frac{1}{4\cdot 3}=\frac{1}{12}\).

With \(y\) heads in \(n\) spins, posterior is \(\theta\mid y\sim \Be(1+y,\;1+n-y)\).

Step 2: Posterior variance. For \(\Be(a,b)\), \(\Var=\frac{ab}{(a+b)^2(a+b+1)}\). Here \(a=1+y\), \(b=1+n-y\), \(a+b=2+n\), \(a+b+1=3+n\). So

\[ \Var(\theta\mid y)=\frac{(1+y)(1+n-y)}{(2+n)^2(3+n)}. \]

Step 3: Factor and bound. Write

\[ \Var(\theta\mid y)=\left(\frac{1+y}{2+n}\right)\left(\frac{1+n-y}{2+n}\right)\left(\frac{1}{3+n}\right). \]

Let \(u=\frac{1+y}{2+n}\in(0,1)\). Then \(\frac{1+n-y}{2+n}=1-u\). The product \(u(1-u)\) is maximized at \(u=1/2\), with maximum \(1/4\). Also \(\frac{1}{3+n}\le \frac{1}{3}\) for \(n\ge0\), and in fact \(<1/3\) for \(n\ge1\).

Therefore \(\Var(\theta\mid y) < \frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{3}=\frac{1}{12}=\Var(\theta)\).

Intuition
Observing data should reduce your uncertainty about \(\theta\). The Uniform prior is maximally uncertain over [0,1] in this Beta family; any Binomial data update makes the posterior more concentrated.

Question 2 — Conceptual True/False 8 marks

These test whether you understand the concepts, not just formulas. The official solutions are short; below explains why each answer is true or false.

Question statement

State whether the following sentences are true or false:

a) The asymptotic posterior distribution (obtained as the number of data points \(\to\infty\)) depends on the maximum likelihood estimator.

b) Improper prior distributions are proportional to 1 (\(\pi(\theta)\propto 1\)) over the support of the parameter.

c) It is desirable to have an acceptance probability as high as possible in a Metropolis-Hastings sampler.

d) If a Markov chain Monte Carlo sampler converges to a stationary distribution, then it is irreducible (i.e. it can visit all parts of the state space with positive probability).

e) Jeffreys' prior \(\pi_J(\theta)\) is invariant to one-to-one transformations of \(\theta\).

f) Improper priors can be used without causing problems in regular Bayes Factors if they are used for parameters that are present in both models.

g) de Finetti's Theorem allows one form of dependence between random variables (exchangeability) to be expressed through a hierarchical model.

h) The mean of the weights obtained through importance sampling is an estimate of the target model's normalisation constant.

Teaching solution
PartAnswerWhyWhat to remember
a True Under regular conditions (Bernstein–von Mises), the posterior is asymptotically normal centered near the MLE \(\hat\theta_n\) with variance related to inverse Fisher information: roughly \(\theta\mid x_{1:n}\approx \N(\hat\theta_n, I_n(\hat\theta_n)^{-1})\). So the asymptotic posterior location/shape is described using the MLE. The prior gets washed out by large data; likelihood/MLE governs asymptotic location.
b False A flat prior \(\pi(\theta)\propto 1\) is one example of an improper prior, but not all improper priors are flat. Example: \(\pi(\sigma^2)\propto 1/\sigma^2\) on \(\R^+\) is improper and not constant. Improper means it does not integrate to a finite constant; it can have many shapes.
c False Very high acceptance often means proposals are too small/local: the chain moves slowly and explores poorly (high autocorrelation, poor mixing). Very low acceptance can also be bad. You want a good tradeoff. High acceptance is not the goal; good mixing/ESS is.
d False A chain can converge to a stationary distribution on only a subset of the state space if it is reducible and started in that subset. Irreducibility is a property needed for convergence to the target from general starting points, not implied the other way around by this statement. Convergence in some cases does not guarantee the chain can visit all positive-probability regions.
e True Jeffreys prior is \(\pi_J(\theta)\propto \sqrt{|I(\theta)|}\). Under a one-to-one transform \(\phi=h(\theta)\), the Fisher information transforms so that the induced prior is the same Jeffreys prior in the new parameterization. This invariance is its defining motivation. Jeffreys prior is reparameterization-invariant.
f False Even if the same parameter appears in both models, using improper priors can make the marginal likelihoods ill-defined because arbitrary constants may not cancel in the Bayes factor. The safe statement is the opposite: improper priors cause problems in regular Bayes factors unless handled very carefully (e.g., common parameters with proper conditional structure, or fractional/partial Bayes factors). Ordinary Bayes factors and improper priors are dangerous; do not assume constants cancel.
g True De Finetti's theorem: an infinite exchangeable sequence can be represented as conditionally i.i.d. given some latent parameter \(\theta\), with \(\theta\) drawn from a prior. This is exactly a hierarchical/Bayesian representation. Exchangeability \(\leftrightarrow\) conditional i.i.d. given latent parameter.
h True If \(w(x)=\tilde f(x)/g(x)\) where \(\tilde f = Z f\) is unnormalized target and \(x_i\sim g\), then \(\E_g[w(X)]=\int \frac{\tilde f(x)}{g(x)}g(x)\d x=\int \tilde f(x)\d x=Z\). The sample mean of weights estimates \(Z\). Mean unnormalized importance weight estimates the normalizing constant.
Exam tip
For T/F questions, if a statement uses absolute words like "always", "as high as possible", or "proportional to 1 are improper priors", be suspicious and test with a counterexample.

Question 3 — Sampling Algorithms 23 marks

This question moves from basic Monte Carlo sampling to MCMC. The key proofs are: rejection sampling gives the right distribution, and MH satisfies detailed balance.

Question statement

Suppose we wish to sample from the distribution \(f(x)\).

a) [10 marks] Let \(X\) be a random variable from any distribution \(g(x)\) such that for some value \(K\), \(f(x)\le K g(x),\forall x\).

i) [5 marks] Give the algorithm for rejection sampling. ii) [5 marks] Prove that the rejection algorithm gives samples distributed according to \(f(x)\). (Hint: Construct the distribution function of accepted samples and show that it is \(F(x)\)).

b) [13 marks] The Metropolis-Hastings algorithm is a very flexible tool for sampling from complex distributions \(f(x)\).

i) [5 marks] Give a brief outline of the algorithm, clearly explaining any notations you use. Note: \(\alpha(x,y)=\min\left(1,\frac{f(y)Q(y,x)}{f(x)Q(x,y)}\right)\). ii) [5 marks] Demonstrate how the reversibility condition is enforced in MH. iii) [1 mark] Give an example of a Metropolis-Hastings sampler that is reducible. iv) [2 marks] Under what condition is MH equivalent to Gibbs sampler? Demonstrate that this is the case.

Q3(a) — Rejection sampling algorithm + proof

What this is testing
You need both the procedure and the correctness proof. The proof is a standard conditional-probability calculation.
Teaching solution

Q3(a)(i) Algorithm. We want samples from target \(f(x)\) but it may be hard to sample directly. We choose an easy proposal \(g(x)\) and constant \(K\) with envelope \(f(x)\le K g(x)\) everywhere.

  1. Simulate \(x^* \sim g(x)\).
  2. Simulate \(u^* \sim \Unif(0, K g(x^*))\) (equivalently \(u\sim\Unif(0,1)\) and compare to \(f(x^*)/(Kg(x^*))\)).
  3. Accept \(x^*\) if \(u^* \le f(x^*)\); otherwise reject and repeat from step 1.

Q3(a)(ii) Proof of correctness. Let \(A\) be the event "sample accepted". Define acceptance probability given proposal \(X=x\) as \(h(x)=\P(A\mid X=x)=\frac{f(x)}{K g(x)}\), which is in \([0,1]\) by the envelope condition.

For any value \(x\), consider the CDF of accepted samples:

\[ \begin{aligned} \P(X\le x \mid A) &= \frac{\P(X\le x, A)}{\P(A)} \\ &= \frac{\int_{-\infty}^{x} \P(A\mid X=y)g(y)\,\d y}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \P(A\mid X=y)g(y)\,\d y} \\ &= \frac{\int_{-\infty}^{x} h(y)g(y)\,\d y}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} h(y)g(y)\,\d y}. \end{aligned} \]

Substitute \(h(y)g(y)=\frac{f(y)}{Kg(y)}g(y)=\frac{f(y)}{K}\):

\[ \P(X\le x\mid A)=\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{x} f(y)/K\,\d y}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(y)/K\,\d y}=\frac{\int_{-\infty}^{x} f(y)\,\d y}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(y)\,\d y}=F(x), \]

assuming \(f\) is normalized (if unnormalized, the same ratio gives normalized target). Thus accepted samples have CDF \(F\), i.e. density \(f\).

Why this proof works
The proposal \(g\) is deliberately canceled by the acceptance probability. You sample more often from regions where \(g\) is large, but accept them less often when \(f/g\) is small. The two effects balance exactly to produce \(f\).

Q3(b) — Metropolis-Hastings proof-heavy

What this is testing
MH algorithm mechanics, the detailed-balance/reversibility argument, and the connection between Gibbs and MH.
Teaching solution

Q3(b)(i) Algorithm outline

Let \(f(x)\) be the target distribution (possibly known up to a constant). Let \(Q(x,y)\) be a proposal density/mass for proposing \(y\) from current state \(x\).

  1. Initialize \(x^{(1)}\). Set \(t=1\).
  2. Propose a candidate \(y\sim Q(x^{(t)},\cdot)\).
  3. Compute acceptance probability \(\alpha(x^{(t)},y)=\min\left(1,\frac{f(y)Q(y,x^{(t)})}{f(x^{(t)})Q(x^{(t)},y)}\right)\).
  4. Draw \(U\sim\Unif(0,1)\). If \(U\le\alpha(x^{(t)},y)\), set \(x^{(t+1)}=y\); otherwise set \(x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}\).
  5. Increment \(t\) and repeat until enough samples collected.

Q3(b)(ii) How reversibility is enforced

We want the chain to have \(f\) as a stationary distribution. A sufficient condition is detailed balance/reversibility:

\[ f(x)P(x,y)=f(y)P(y,x),\quad \forall x\neq y. \]

For a proposed move, the transition density is \(P(x,y)=Q(x,y)\alpha(x,y)\) (for \(y\neq x\)). So detailed balance requires

\[ f(x)Q(x,y)\alpha(x,y)=f(y)Q(y,x)\alpha(y,x). \]

Consider two cases. WLOG suppose \(f(x)Q(x,y)\ge f(y)Q(y,x)\), meaning moves \(y\to x\) are relatively under-proposed. To make the right side as large as possible, set \(\alpha(y,x)=1\). Then solve for \(\alpha(x,y)\):

\[ \alpha(x,y)=\frac{f(y)Q(y,x)}{f(x)Q(x,y)}. \]

In the opposite case the ratio is \(\ge1\), so we cap at 1. Combining both cases gives

\[ \alpha(x,y)=\min\left(1,\frac{f(y)Q(y,x)}{f(x)Q(x,y)}\right). \]

You can also see the symmetry directly:

\[ f(x)Q(x,y)\alpha(x,y)=\min\left(f(x)Q(x,y),\;f(y)Q(y,x)\right), \]

which is symmetric in \(x,y\), proving detailed balance.

Q3(b)(iii) Reducible MH example

A simple example: an independent proposal \(Q(x,y)=g(y)\) whose support does not cover the support of \(f\). For instance, if \(f\) has positive mass on \((-\infty,0)\) and \((0,\infty)\) but \(g(y)\) only proposes \(y>0\), then the chain can never visit negative states. The chain is reducible because part of the target's support is unreachable.

Q3(b)(iv) Gibbs as a special case of MH

Gibbs sampling is MH when the proposal for updating component \(\theta_j\) is its full conditional distribution: \(Q(\theta,\theta^*)=\pi(\theta_j^*\mid \theta_{-j},y)\), keeping other components fixed. Plug into MH ratio:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\pi(\theta^*\mid y)Q(\theta^*,\theta)}{\pi(\theta\mid y)Q(\theta,\theta^*)} &= \frac{\pi(\theta_j^*\mid\theta_{-j},y)\pi(\theta_{-j}\mid y)\;\pi(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j},y)} {\pi(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j},y)\pi(\theta_{-j}\mid y)\;\pi(\theta_j^*\mid\theta_{-j},y)} = 1. \end{aligned} \]

So \(\alpha=1\): Gibbs proposals are always accepted.

Question 4 — Mixture Models 15 marks

Mixtures test whether you understand latent-variable augmentation, full conditionals, and identifiability. The key trick is introducing indicators to turn a sum inside a product into a product.

Question statement

Suppose we wish to model observed data \(y=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)^\top\) by a mixture distribution with \(M\) components. Let \(\omega_m\), \(m=1,\ldots,M\), denote the weight of the \(m\)-th mixture component density \(f_m(y\mid\theta_m)\), with parameters \(\theta_m\) and \(\sum_{m=1}^M \omega_m=1\).

a) [2 marks] Give an expression for the likelihood function of \(f(y\mid\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_M)\) without using indicator variables.

b) [2 marks] Consider indicator variables \(z_{im}\), where \(z_{im}=1\) if \(y_i\) is drawn from mixture component \(m\), and \(z_{im}=0\) otherwise. Write \(z_m=(z_{1m},\ldots,z_{nm})^\top\), \(z=(z_1,\ldots,z_M)\), \(\theta=(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_M)\). Provide an expression for the likelihood function for \(f(y\mid z,\theta)\) which does involve the indicator variables.

c) [5 marks] Suppose that \(z_m=0\) for some \(m\). Write down explicitly the form of the full posterior conditional \(f(\theta_m\mid y,z_m=0)\). How might this influence your (conditional) prior specification for mixture component \(m\), \(\pi(\theta_m\mid\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_{m-1},\theta_{m+1},\ldots,\theta_M)\)?

d) [6 marks] Describe how parameter non-identifiability naturally arises in the posterior distribution for mixture models, and the problems this may pose for inference. Suggest two methods for avoiding a non-identifiable posterior in the mixture model setting.

Q4(a) — Likelihood without indicators easy

Teaching solution

Each observation \(y_i\) comes from component \(m\) with probability \(\omega_m\), giving density \(f_m(y_i\mid\theta_m)\). Marginalizing over the unknown component label,

\[ p(y_i\mid \theta,\omega)=\sum_{m=1}^M \omega_m f_m(y_i\mid\theta_m). \]

Assuming observations are conditionally independent given parameters, the likelihood is the product:

\[ f(y\mid\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_M,\omega)=\prod_{i=1}^n \left[\sum_{m=1}^M \omega_m f_m(y_i\mid\theta_m)\right]. \]

Why this is awkward: the sum is inside the product. Expanding gives \(M^n\) terms, which is computationally horrible and algebraically messy.

Q4(b) — Likelihood with indicators easy

Teaching solution

Let \(z_{im}=1\) if observation \(i\) is assigned to component \(m\), else 0. For each \(i\), exactly one \(z_{im}=1\) and \(\sum_m z_{im}=1\). Conditional on \(z\), the component is known, so

\[ f(y_i\mid z_i,\theta)=\prod_{m=1}^M f_m(y_i\mid\theta_m)^{z_{im}}. \]

(Only the factor with \(z_{im}=1\) survives; the others are raised to power 0.) Therefore

\[ f(y\mid z,\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n\prod_{m=1}^M \left[f_m(y_i\mid\theta_m)\right]^{z_{im}}. \]

If weights are included in the joint complete-data model, you may also see \(\prod_i\prod_m [\omega_m f_m(y_i\mid\theta_m)]^{z_{im}}\). The question asks for \(f(y\mid z,\theta)\), so the component-density form above is the direct answer; be clear about conditioning.

Q4(c) — Empty component conditional important

Teaching solution

If \(z_m=0\) (the zero vector), then no observation is allocated to component \(m\). Look at the full conditional:

\[ f(\theta_m\mid y,z,\theta_{-m},\omega) \propto f(y\mid z,\theta)\,\pi(\theta_m\mid\theta_{-m}). \]

In the likelihood, \(\theta_m\) appears only through factors where \(z_{im}=1\). If all \(z_{im}=0\), there is no likelihood contribution from component \(m\). Hence

\[ f(\theta_m\mid y,z_m=0,\theta_{-m}) \propto \pi(\theta_m\mid\theta_{-m}). \]

Implication for prior specification: the posterior conditional for an empty component is exactly its (conditional) prior. Therefore this prior must be proper. If you use an improper prior for \(\theta_m\), the full conditional/posterior can become improper when component \(m\) is empty — a real problem in MCMC.

Exam takeaway
Empty mixture component \(\Rightarrow\) no data inform that component \(\Rightarrow\) posterior = prior \(\Rightarrow\) prior must be proper.

Q4(d) — Label switching / non-identifiability conceptual

Teaching solution

How it arises. The mixture likelihood is

\[ \prod_{i=1}^n \left[\sum_{m=1}^M \omega_m f_m(y_i\mid\theta_m)\right]. \]

If you permute component labels — e.g. swap component 1 and 2, including their weights and parameters — the sum is the same. So different parameter labelings produce identical likelihood values. With symmetric priors, the posterior inherits this symmetry.

Problems for inference.

  • Component-specific summaries (mean of component 1, probability \(y_i\) belongs to component 1) become meaningless if "component 1" keeps changing identity.
  • MCMC chains may switch labels across iterations, making ergodic averages for component parameters misleading.
  • Posterior multimodality may be an artifact of labeling, not genuinely different explanations.

Two methods to avoid/fix it (any two good ones):

  • Ordering constraints: impose an order such as \(\mu_1 < \mu_2 < \cdots < \mu_M\), or order by weights/variances. This breaks symmetry but can be artificial.
  • Informative/non-exchangeable priors: choose priors that distinguish components in a scientifically meaningful way, preventing label switching.
  • Post-processing relabeling: after sampling, permute labels in each draw to minimize a relabeling loss.

Question 5 — Bayes Factors & Model Comparison 25 marks

This question tests marginal likelihood, prior sensitivity, and fractional Bayes factors. Q5(c) is marked non-examinable in the sample exam, but I include a full teaching explanation as optional.

Question statement

a) [6 marks] Provide a definition of the Bayes Factor, being careful to define any notation that you use. Show how the ordinary Bayes Factor can be sensitive to the value of the prior \(\pi_m(\theta_m)\), at \(\theta_m=\hat\theta_m\), where \(\hat\theta_m\) is the maximum likelihood estimator of \(\theta_m\) under model \(m\), even when \(\pi_m\) is proper and informative, but where the influence of the data is strong.

b) [6 marks] Define the fractional Bayes Factor with training fraction \(b\in[0,1]\). Show how the fractional Bayes Factor overcomes the problem of improper prior distributions.

c) [13 marks] Can skip this question, contains non-examinable material. (Explained below as optional.)

Q5(a) — Bayes factor definition and prior sensitivity core

Teaching solution

Definition. For two models \(M_1, M_2\) with data \(x\), parameters \(\theta_1,\theta_2\), likelihoods \(f_1(x\mid\theta_1),f_2(x\mid\theta_2)\), and priors \(\pi_1(\theta_1),\pi_2(\theta_2)\), the marginal likelihood (evidence) for model \(m\) is

\[ m_m(x)=p(x\mid M_m)=\int_{\Theta_m} f_m(x\mid\theta_m)\pi_m(\theta_m)\,\d\theta_m. \]

The Bayes factor in favor of \(M_1\) over \(M_2\) is

\[ B_{12}=\frac{m_1(x)}{m_2(x)}=\frac{\int f_1(x\mid\theta_1)\pi_1(\theta_1)\,\d\theta_1}{\int f_2(x\mid\theta_2)\pi_2(\theta_2)\,\d\theta_2}. \]

It updates prior odds to posterior odds: \(\frac{P(M_1\mid x)}{P(M_2\mid x)}=B_{12}\frac{P(M_1)}{P(M_2)}\).

Why it is sensitive to \(\pi_m(\hat\theta_m)\) even with strong data.

When the data are strong, the likelihood \(f_m(x\mid\theta_m)\) is concentrated around its MLE \(\hat\theta_m\) and is negligible elsewhere. If the prior is relatively smooth/informative over that narrow region, \(\pi_m(\theta_m)\approx \pi_m(\hat\theta_m)\) over the region where likelihood is large. Then

\[ m_m(x)=\int f_m(x\mid\theta_m)\pi_m(\theta_m)\,\d\theta_m \approx \pi_m(\hat\theta_m)\int f_m(x\mid\theta_m)\,\d\theta_m. \]

Thus the marginal likelihood is approximately proportional to the prior density at the MLE. A perturbation that changes \(\pi_m(\hat\theta_m)\) by a factor changes \(m_m(x)\), and hence \(B_{12}\), by roughly the same factor. This is true even if the posterior for \(\theta_m\) itself is stable, because the Bayes factor depends on the prior-normalized integrated evidence, not just the posterior location.

Key distinction
Strong data usually make posterior inference within a model robust to the prior, but Bayes factors between models can remain sensitive to prior scale/height.

Q5(b) — Fractional Bayes factor core

Teaching solution

Definition. The fractional Bayes factor with training fraction \(b\in(0,1]\) is

\[ B_{12}^b = \frac{f_1^*(x)}{f_2^*(x)}, \qquad f_m^*(x)=\frac{\int f_m(x\mid\theta_m)\pi_m(\theta_m)\,\d\theta_m}{\int [f_m(x\mid\theta_m)]^b \pi_m(\theta_m)\,\d\theta_m}. \]

How it handles improper priors. Suppose \(\pi_m(\theta_m)=c_m g_m(\theta_m)\) is improper, where \(c_m\) is an arbitrary undefined normalizing constant. Substitute into \(f_m^*(x)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f_m^*(x)&=\frac{\int f_m(x\mid\theta_m)c_m g_m(\theta_m)\,\d\theta_m}{\int [f_m(x\mid\theta_m)]^b c_m g_m(\theta_m)\,\d\theta_m} \\ &=\frac{c_m}{c_m}\cdot \frac{\int f_m(x\mid\theta_m)g_m(\theta_m)\,\d\theta_m}{\int [f_m(x\mid\theta_m)]^b g_m(\theta_m)\,\d\theta_m}. \end{aligned} \]

The arbitrary \(c_m\) cancels. Moreover, the denominator can be viewed as producing a normalized "trained" distribution:

\[ f_m^*(x)=\int f_m(x\mid\theta_m)^{1-b} \underbrace{\frac{[f_m(x\mid\theta_m)]^b \pi_m(\theta_m)}{\int [f_m(x\mid\theta_m)]^b \pi_m(\theta_m)\,\d\theta_m}}_{\text{proper trained prior/posterior}}\,\d\theta_m. \]

So fraction \(b\) of the likelihood trains the prior into a proper distribution, and the remaining \(1-b\) is used for model comparison. This removes the arbitrary-constant problem.

Q5(c) — Optional/non-examinable worked example non-examinable

Sample exam note
The sample exam says: "Can skip this question, contains non-examinable material." Still useful practice for Bayes-factor mechanics.
Teaching solution (optional)

Data \(y_1,\ldots,y_n\). Test \(M_1: y_i\sim \N(0,1)\) vs \(M_2: y_i\sim \N(\theta,1)\), \(i=1,\ldots,n\), with improper uniform prior \(\pi(\theta)=c\), \(-\infty<\theta<\infty\).

(i) Spiegelhalter–Smith imaginary training sample to set \(c\)

Minimal sample is one observation \(y\). Under \(M_1\), \(f_1(y)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-y^2/2}\). Under \(M_2\),

\[ f_2(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} c(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-(y-\theta)^2/2}\,\d\theta = c(2\pi)^{-1/2}\sqrt{2\pi}=c. \]

(The normal kernel in \(\theta\) integrates to \(\sqrt{2\pi}\).) Thus \(B_{12}=\frac{f_1(y)}{f_2(y)}=\frac{(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-y^2/2}}{c}\). This is maximized at \(y=0\), giving maximal support to the simpler model: \(B_{12}^{max}=\frac{(2\pi)^{-1/2}}{c}\). Set \(B_{12}=1\) for this imaginary sample: \(c=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\).

(ii) Bayes factor for actual data

For observed \(y=(y_1,\ldots,y_n)\), under \(M_1\): \(f_1(y)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum y_i^2\right)\).

Under \(M_2\), with \(c=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\):

\[ f_2(y)=\int (2\pi)^{-1/2}(2\pi)^{-n/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\theta)^2\right)\,\d\theta. \]

Use the sum-of-squares decomposition \(\sum (y_i-\theta)^2=\sum (y_i-\bar y)^2+n(\bar y-\theta)^2\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f_2(y)&=(2\pi)^{-(n+1)/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum (y_i-\bar y)^2\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{n}{2}(\theta-\bar y)^2\right)\,\d\theta \\ &=(2\pi)^{-(n+1)/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum (y_i-\bar y)^2\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{n}} \\ &=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}\sqrt{n}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum (y_i-\bar y)^2\right). \end{aligned} \]

Now form the Bayes factor:

\[ \begin{aligned} B_{12}&=\frac{f_1(y)}{f_2(y)}=\sqrt{n}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[\sum y_i^2-\sum (y_i-\bar y)^2\right]\right). \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\sum y_i^2-\sum (y_i-\bar y)^2=n\bar y^2\),

\[ B_{12}=\sqrt{n}\exp\left(-\frac{n}{2}\bar y^2\right). \]

So \(a(n)=\sqrt{n}\), \(b(n)=-n/2\).

(iii) Behavior as \(n\to\infty\)

If \(\bar y\) stays away from 0 (true mean nonzero), the exponential \(\exp(-n\bar y^2/2)\) decays much faster than \(\sqrt{n}\) grows, so \(B_{12}\to 0\), favoring \(M_2\). If \(\bar y\to 0\) in the relevant way (data consistent with \(\theta=0\)), the exponential term goes to 1 and \(B_{12}\sim\sqrt{n}\to\infty\), favoring \(M_1\). This matches intuition: more data gives decisive evidence for the correct model.
